# 1. Executive summary

## 1.1 The business concept

Automotive Supply Network (ASN) will be a unified digital platform connecting:

- Automotive spare-parts manufacturers and distributors.
- Wholesalers and retailers.
- Independent garages and workshops.
- Fleet operators.
- Individual vehicle owners.
- Independent delivery and logistics providers.

The platform will allow customers to discover and purchase automotive parts, suppliers to manage their businesses, suppliers to trade with each other, and logistics partners to fulfil deliveries through one coordinated network.

The central idea is simple:

A buyer should not need to know which distributor has the required part, which supplier can source it or which delivery partner can transport it. The platform should coordinate the entire transaction.

## 1.2 What we are building

## Four connected business pillars

01\. Automotive Marketplace

Product discovery, comparison, purchasing, RFQs and order tracking for B2B and B2C customers.

02\. Supplier OS

Free basic software for inventory, quotations, orders, customers and procurement.

03\. Supplier Supply Network

Wholesale transactions, sourcing, stockout resolution and inter-supplier commerce.

04\. Platform-managed Logistics

Independent delivery partners coordinated through a common allocation, routing and tracking system.

# 2. Market opportunity and positioning

## 2.1 The problem

Automotive aftermarket procurement is fragmented.

A garage may need a brake assembly, filter, sensor or suspension component. It may have to contact multiple shops and distributors to locate the correct part.

The process often involves:

- Calling suppliers individually.
- Sending photographs over WhatsApp.
- Searching different part numbers.
- Manually comparing prices.
- Confirming compatibility.
- Negotiating availability.
- Arranging pickup or delivery.
- Following up on invoices and payment.

This creates friction for both buyers and suppliers.

## 2.2 Problems for each participant

| Participant      | Problem                           | ASN solution                |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Garage           | Difficult to locate parts quickly | Unified search and sourcing |
| Retailer         | Stockouts and excess inventory    | Inter-supplier trading      |
| Distributor      | Limited digital visibility        | Supplier OS and marketplace |
| Wholesaler       | Fragmented buyer network          | Wholesale marketplace       |
| Fleet operator   | Procurement and downtime          | Centralised procurement     |
| Vehicle owner    | Uncertain compatibility           | Vehicle-based discovery     |
| Delivery partner | Fragmented delivery demand        | Coordinated assignments     |

## 2.3 Positioning

ASN should not position itself as merely an online spare-parts shop.

It should be positioned as:

An automotive aftermarket commerce and procurement network.

Its competitive strengths should be built around:

1. Supplier network density.
2. Reliable product identification and fitment.
3. Live stock confirmation.
4. Inter-supplier sourcing.
5. Multi-partner logistics coordination.
6. Transparent transaction economics.

These capabilities must be proven through operations; they should not be advertised as guaranteed until validated.

# 6. Network-first 60-minute logistics architecture

Core operating policy

This is one of the most important parts of the platform.

The delivery system must search the wider network before rejecting an order.

## 6.1 Delivery promise

- Express: 30–45 minutes.
- Quick: 46–60 minutes.
- Maximum customer-facing quick-delivery SLA: 60 minutes.
- Internal target: 50–55 minutes.
- No paid delayed-delivery option within the core quick-delivery proposition.

This is a service commitment subject to the published eligibility and exception rules, not a claim that every location and order is always serviceable.

## 6.2 Network-first workflow

## Network availability engine

1. Initial supplier search

   Find local suppliers with potentially suitable stock.
2. Supplier confirmation

   Confirm quantity, product identity, fitment, price and preparation time.
3. Expanded supplier search

   Search additional suppliers if the first option is unsuitable.
4. Initial logistics search

   Find a suitable available delivery partner.
5. Expanded logistics search

   Search additional partners, vehicle types and service areas.
6. Multi-partner route optimisation

   Consider multiple pickups, transfers and handovers.
7. 60-minute eligibility check

   Validate the complete fulfilment journey.
8. Buyer confirmation and payment

   Display the actual seller, product, price and delivery estimate before payment.

## 6.3 Delivery time calculation

\\[ T\_{\text{delivery}} = T\_{\text{preparation}}+ T\_{\text{pickup}}+ T\_{\text{collection}}+ T\_{\text{transfer}}+ T\_{\text{last mile}}+ T\_{\text{handover}} \\]

The route engine must calculate actual sequential and overlapping legs correctly, rather than simply adding all durations.

Use current or recent traffic information, partner capacity, preparation time, package size, number of pickups and handover requirements.

Reject the quick-delivery checkout if the complete predicted journey exceeds 60 minutes.

## 6.4 Five-minute reservation policy

| Parameter                 | Default                         |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Stock reservation         | 5 minutes                       |
| Configurable alternatives | 3, 5 or 7 minutes               |
| Partner allocation        | Temporary non-dispatch hold     |
| Reservation expiry        | Automatic                       |
| Stock commitment          | Successful payment              |
| SLA clock start           | Successful payment confirmation |

The reservation period is not included in the 60-minute delivery clock.

Before payment:

- Supplier confirms stock.
- Supplier confirms preparation time.
- Partner confirms capacity.
- Route passes the 60-minute check.
- Buyer approves any changed price or alternative.

After payment:

- Inventory is committed.
- Supplier order is confirmed.
- Delivery dispatch begins.
- SLA tracking starts.

## 6.5 Fallback rules

If the first supplier is unavailable:

- Search another supplier.
- Expand the service area.
- Search compatible alternatives only with buyer approval.

If the first delivery partner is unavailable:

- Search other partners.
- Consider a different vehicle type.
- Consider multi-partner routing.

If no arrangement qualifies:

1. Offer a different eligible supplier.
2. Offer a compatible alternative, subject to fitment confirmation and buyer approval.
3. Offer nearby collection if available.
4. Offer an unpaid sourcing request.

For a multi-supplier basket, default to all-or-nothing quick delivery. Offer a separate partial order if one component is ineligible.

# 7. Revenue and commercial architecture

## 7.1 Revenue model

ASN is commission-first, with no mandatory supplier subscription.

| Revenue source                | Illustrative initial range |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| B2B marketplace               | 3–5%                       |
| B2C marketplace               | 8–10%                      |
| Supplier-to-supplier          | 1–3%                       |
| Logistics coordination margin | 5–15% of delivery charge   |
| Basic Supplier OS             | Free                       |

These are planning assumptions, not final market-validated rates. Admin can configure rules and negotiate supplier-specific agreements.

## 7.2 Commission engine

A central Commission & Settlement Engine (CCSE) must calculate charges across all four pillars.

Rule precedence:

1. Supplier-specific negotiated agreement.
2. Transaction type.
3. Buyer/supplier category.
4. Vehicle/product category.
5. Default rule.

### Commission principles

- Only one applicable parts commission on the same underlying sale.
- Wholesale purchase and subsequent retail sale are separate transactions.
- Logistics margin is separately calculated.
- Fee payer and fee basis are disclosed before order acceptance.
- Refunds trigger proportional reversals.
- Failed or cancelled orders do not incur normal completed-sale commission, subject to disclosed cancellation/recovery costs.
- Historical transactions preserve their original rate snapshot.
- Admin changes are audited.
- High-impact changes require additional approval.

## 7.3 Example transaction economics

Assume a buyer purchases parts worth ₹5,000.

| Component                                               | Example |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------- |
| Product value                                           | ₹5,000  |
| Parts commission at 4%                                  | ₹200    |
| Delivery charge                                         | ₹100    |
| Logistics coordination margin at 10% of delivery charge | ₹10     |
| Illustrative platform revenue                           | ₹210    |

This is before applicable taxes, payment costs, support costs, delivery-related costs and other operating expenses.

It is not a profit calculation.

## 7.4 Financial infrastructure

Maintain:

- Payment transaction records.
- Seller allocations.
- Commission calculations.
- Refund records.
- Commission reversals.
- Partner payables.
- Settlement batches.
- Reconciliation records.
- Append-only financial ledger.

Do not create an independent unlicensed wallet or assume a payment gateway automatically supports compliant marketplace split settlements.

## 12.2 Financial model structure

The business should be forecast using separate revenue streams.

\\[ \text{Platform Revenue} = R\_{\text{B2B}}+ R\_{\text{B2C}}+ R\_{\text{Wholesale}}+ R\_{\text{Logistics}} \\]

Contribution:

\\[ \text{Contribution} = \text{Platform Revenue} - \text{Variable Costs} \\]

Variable costs include:

- Payment processing.
- Refund and dispute losses.
- Customer support per order.
- Logistics subsidies, if any.
- Incentives.
- Variable cloud and communication costs.

Fixed costs include:

- Employees.
- Development.
- Hosting baseline.
- Legal and compliance.
- Office and administration.
- Marketing overhead.

Start with one concentrated cluster, not all of Mumbai.

## 13.2 Initial catalogue strategy

Support all three vehicle segments in the architecture, but start with manageable categories.

Prioritise common, frequently purchased products with reliable part identification and reasonable handling requirements.

Do not attempt to catalogue every automotive component before launch.

## 13.3 Expansion sequence

1. Phase 1 — Mumbai pilot

   Validate suppliers, stock, order workflows and delivery operations.
2. Phase 2 — Mumbai expansion

   Increase supplier density and add adjacent service areas.
3. Phase 3 — Other metropolitan markets

4. Phase 4 — Regional network

   Build cross-city wholesale sourcing and broader distribution capabilities.
5. Phase 5 — National infrastructure

   Scale supplier integrations, fleet procurement, APIs and advanced catalogue intelligence.

# 15. Testing and acceptance criteria

The developer should not consider the platform complete merely because all screens render.

Every workflow must be tested.

## 15.1 Marketplace testing

- Buyer can search by vehicle and part number.
- Product fitment is correctly displayed.
- Buyer can compare suppliers.
- RFQs reach relevant suppliers.
- Multi-supplier checkout creates separate seller orders.
- Invoices and refunds are correctly associated.

## 15.2 Supplier testing

- Supplier can upload stock.
- Supplier can edit stock and price.
- Buyer marketplace reflects stock changes.
- Supplier can accept or reject orders.
- Concurrent buyers cannot reserve the same last unit.

## 15.3 Logistics testing

- First partner unavailable, alternate partner found.
- First supplier unavailable, alternate supplier found.
- Multi-pickup route is correctly evaluated.
- Multi-partner handover is recorded.
- Complete predicted ETA over 60 minutes blocks quick checkout.
- Partner cancellation triggers reassignment.
- Reservation expiry releases stock and partner holds.
- Delivery clock starts only after successful payment.

## 15.4 Financial testing

- Commission rules are applied correctly.
- No duplicate commission is charged.
- Refunds reverse applicable commissions.
- Settlement matches seller allocation.
- Payment webhooks are idempotent.
- Financial ledger corrections preserve history.

## 15.5 Security testing

- Suppliers cannot access another supplier's private data.
- Delivery partners see only assigned shipments.
- Buyers cannot access admin functions.
- Staff permissions are enforced.
- Sensitive admin changes are audited.
- Payment and personal data are protected.

## Final product definition

The final product is one integrated automotive commerce platform with five user experiences:

- Buyer marketplace.
- Supplier operating system.
- Supplier wholesale network.
- Logistics partner application.
- Admin control centre.

All are powered by one shared catalogue, inventory infrastructure, order engine, commission engine and logistics network.

The platform does not own stock, warehouses or delivery vehicles at launch. It coordinates independent suppliers and logistics providers through a common digital system.

The core competitive proposition is not simply selling automotive parts online. It is connecting fragmented automotive supply, procurement and fulfilment into one coordinated network.

## 20. Immediate execution priorities

I would now give your developer these five priorities in order:

1. Audit and finalise the technical architecture and existing starter code.
2. Build the database, authentication, business accounts and supplier inventory.
3. Connect the buyer marketplace to actual supplier listings and RFQs.
4. Build the reservation, network-first availability and 60-minute logistics engine.
5. Integrate payments, commissions, settlements and operational administration.

Do not spend months perfecting a visually impressive homepage while the supplier inventory and transaction infrastructure remain disconnected.

The real value of ASN will come from the network operating reliably.
